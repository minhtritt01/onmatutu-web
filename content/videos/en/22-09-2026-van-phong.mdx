Worn out for a month straight. Shallow sleep, a heavy head before the day even starts, and a full balance of leave days sitting untouched in the system. Every night she opens her email, types two lines asking for time off, and deletes them. Not because she isn't allowed — but because the same question blocks her every time: if I'm gone, who does this?

![Moment 1](/characters/22-09-2026-van-phong-1.png)

The fear was never really about the work. It was the belief that without her, everything would fall apart. Then one day in a meeting she couldn't hear what her boss was saying anymore; her mind had gone blank. That's when she saw it: she was sitting right there and might as well have been absent. She hadn't taken a single day off, and she'd been gone for weeks. That night she hit send before she could change her mind.

![Moment 2](/characters/22-09-2026-van-phong-2.png)

She'd prepared her reasons in advance, braced for questions, for being thought lazy. Her boss approved it in twenty minutes and added one line: go rest, take care of yourself. It turned out he'd seen she was exhausted long ago — she was simply the last person willing to admit it. Nobody had forbidden her to rest. The one saying no was her. For three days she closed the laptop, slept, walked, and opened nothing.

![Moment 3](/characters/22-09-2026-van-phong-3.png)

Back at work, her chair was still there and everything had kept running without her. The thing she feared most — being absent means losing your place — never happened. And what she'd expected to feel like bad news, that the company didn't need her quite that much, turned out to be the best news of the month: it meant she was allowed to be tired, allowed to rest, without carrying the whole office on her back. Next time she's worn down, she'll ask sooner.

> I thought no one could replace me.
> Turns out I was allowed to rest.

How many leave days do you have left — and who are you saving them for?
