On the night of the full moon, the rental block is empty. Every door is padlocked from the
outside; only her room still spills a strip of light. She buys one small mooncake, brews a pot of
tea, and drags a plastic stool out to the balcony.

![Moment 1](/characters/25-09-2026-chung-4.png)

Down in the alley, kids run past with star lanterns, their laughter bouncing off the walls. A few
years ago, that sight would have ruined her whole night. This year she just watches, takes a bite
of cake, pours another cup — and feels quiet inside. No one is coming by, and nothing feels missing.

![Moment 2](/characters/25-09-2026-chung-5.png)

She used to spend every full-moon night lying in bed, scrolling: someone's family feast, someone
else's crowded group photo. The more she scrolled, the lonelier she felt. Only now does she see it
— the sadness never came from being alone. It came from comparing. Tonight she sets her phone face
down on the railing and looks up at the moon.

![Moment 3](/characters/25-09-2026-chung-6.png)

For a few years she dreaded Mid-Autumn so much she'd invite just anyone out, as long as she didn't
have to stay in alone. It's not that she needs her friends any less now — the group chat is still
busy, there's still a plan for the weekend. She's just not afraid of being alone anymore. She takes
a photo of the moon and sends it to her mom.

> No more calling people to feel less sad.
> I see them because I want to.

This full-moon night, who are you sitting with — or are you sitting with yourself?
