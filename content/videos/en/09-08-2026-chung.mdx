Her family eats dinner with the TV on. Nobody watches it, nobody changes the channel, nobody even turns toward it. It's just that if you switched it off, the room would go quiet — and no one in that house has known what to do with the quiet for a very long time.

![Moment 1](/characters/09-08-2026-chung-1.png)

That night was the same. Chopsticks against the rim of a bowl, the TV, and nothing else. Then her mother reached for the remote and pressed it off. For exactly one second. Then pressed it back on. She saw that whole second happen. So it was never that her mother had nothing to say. Her mother was hesitating too — the same hesitation she had — and they had both been doing it for years at the same table.

![Moment 2](/characters/09-08-2026-chung-2.png)

Her family argues. They always have, since she was small. So when she opened the old album, she found exactly what she expected: in every photo everyone stands slightly apart, a gap left in the middle. She flipped quickly, the way you do when you already know what you'll see. Then she reached the last page. Everyone pressed together, an arm around a shoulder, all of them laughing. That photo had been in her house the entire time. Her memory had simply chosen to keep the other ones.

![Moment 3](/characters/09-08-2026-chung-3.png)

Working far from home, three months without a visit. Her mother messaged, *come home this weekend?* — and for the third time she typed the same three words: *I'll see.* She put the phone down, sat on the edge of the bed, and braced for her mother's disappointment. Then the screen lit up once more: *It's fine if you're busy, love.* Her mother had cleared the awkwardness away for her, before she even had time to feel guilty.

> She isn't waiting for an apology.
> She's waiting for me to come home.

No house is perfect, and maybe none of us needs to wait until it is. Between all those unfinished places, how much has been quietly standing there — while we were too busy to notice?
