Five years now, and every morning is the same. The shirt hangs on the hook from the night before, worn pale in one spot at the collar. He wakes before the alarm, sits still for a few minutes in a room that isn't quite light yet, then reaches up and takes it down.

![Moment 1](/characters/30-07-2026-van-phong-1.png)

On the fridge there's a first-grade enrolment notice, held up by a red magnet. He has wanted to quit for a long time — long enough that he can't say exactly when it started. At work his hands open the file on their own, scroll on their own, type on their own. He does all of it, and none of it wrong. It's only that if someone asked him what he likes, he would go quiet. The job doesn't take his time. It takes the part of him that knew what he liked.

![Moment 2](/characters/30-07-2026-van-phong-2.png)

One dinner, his kid asked what he does over there. A question that simple, and he had no answer. Not because it's hard to explain, but because he couldn't find anything in it worth telling. His work badge was off, sitting beside the rice bowl. His kid stayed on the small plastic stool, waiting. What scared him wasn't disappointing anyone — it was the thought of his kid, fifteen years from now, sitting at some desk unable to answer the same question.

![Moment 3](/characters/30-07-2026-van-phong-3.png)

One night around eleven, he finished writing his resignation. Read it through once. Then pulled the scrap paper over and added it up again — school fees, rent — twice, to be sure. He deleted every word of the letter and closed the laptop. It isn't that he doesn't dare. He did the math a long time ago and made peace with the answer. It just still lands as heavy as the first time.

> It's not that I don't dare to leave.
> It's that someone here I can't.

In the corner, his kid's animal night light is still on. Tomorrow he'll button the shirt and walk out again. If anyone asked why, he'd probably just point at that corner of the room.

And you — have you ever asked your parents what they like?
