Rush hour, and the rain starts the moment he leaves the parking garage. Halfway home the engine dies. He is standing in a river of headlights, everyone sliding past, shirt soaked through to the back. The feeling is not fear. It is being stranded — the whole city is going home and he is the one stuck under the rain.

![Moment 1](/characters/16-09-2026-chung-1.png)

Then one bike peels off the stream and pulls over. No questions. The rider gets off and pushes with him. The engine catches, and they turn around and ride off before he can ask a name. Under an awning afterwards, what stays with him is not that he was helped — it is that they did not need him to remember. They saw he was wet. That was all. The next day he passes someone stalled at the kerb and eases off the throttle.

![Moment 2](/characters/16-09-2026-chung-2.png)

One morning his wallet is empty and payday has not come. He stands at the bánh mì cart and cannot bring himself to order. She looks at the open wallet and says: pay me tomorrow. She does not know his name. Sitting on a plastic stool with the iced tea she pours, she tells him that years ago someone did the same for her. She is not being naive. She is passing on something she was given. He comes back the next morning to pay, and buys one more loaf.

![Moment 3](/characters/16-09-2026-chung-3.png)

Three months in the rented room and he has never spoken to the landlady beyond rent. One late night his phone lights up: are you home yet? Not his mother. Her. It turns out she counts the bikes in the courtyard every night, and will not sleep while one is missing. Those three silent months were not nobody noticing — he just had not noticed the person noticing him. He texts back: I'm home, chị.

> Family isn't only who lives in your house.
> It's whoever counts your bike every night.

None of these people are in his contacts. But they are real, and there are more of them than he thought. And you — when was the last time a stranger was kind to you, and do you still remember their face?
