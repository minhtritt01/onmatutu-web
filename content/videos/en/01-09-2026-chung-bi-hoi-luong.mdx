The holiday table is crowded. Bowls stacked on both sides, the rice pot still steaming. Then the question lands, as casual as a greeting: so, how much do you make?

Three days home means three days of this. Every meal someone asks, and every meal there is a small silence right after.

![Moment 1](/characters/01-09-2026-chung-4.png)

Last year she snapped back. Not loudly — just one short sentence. The table went quiet, and her mother put another piece of meat in her bowl without saying anything. This year she tried something else: a smile, then a question of her own about the rice harvest. Her uncle started talking immediately. The earlier question evaporated, as if nobody had asked it at all.

![Moment 2](/characters/01-09-2026-chung-5.png)

The year she snapped, her mother washed the dishes alone that night. Her uncle had gone home; he carried nothing away with him. The person who cleared that table, who wiped down that silence, was her mother. The answer she threw never reached the person who asked — it fell in the middle of the house, and her mother was the one who bent down to pick it up.

![Moment 3](/characters/01-09-2026-chung-6.png)

When she finally asked about the rice, her uncle talked for half an hour. The sacks stacked in the corner of the porch, whether the yield was good, whether prices had gone up. He was never waiting for her number. He was looking for somewhere to start talking, and that question was the only opening he knew.

> He never needed my number.
> He needed someone to listen.

The next evening, she asked first. Not because anything had been forgiven — just because it was easier. And you: the last time someone asked you something that stung, did you stop to wonder what they were actually trying to talk about?
