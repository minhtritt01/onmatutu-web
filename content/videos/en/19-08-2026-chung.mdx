Two years ago, close to midnight, his best friend since high school messaged him: his mother was in hospital and he couldn't cover it. He transferred the money that same night. No paperwork, no due date — you don't do that to a friend.

![Moment 1](/characters/19-08-2026-chung-1.png)

For someone working a technician's job, it wasn't a small amount, but it wasn't beyond him either: a few months of work and he'd have it back. He sent it without thinking twice, because the only thing worth thinking about that night was the woman lying in that hospital room. The money, he figured, his friend would remember on his own. What he didn't expect was that his friend would remember it too well — so well he couldn't face him anymore.

![Moment 2](/characters/19-08-2026-chung-2.png)

At first there were messages promising to pay it back soon. Then fewer. Then none. He texted just to check in, never once mentioning the money, and the messages still sat there unanswered. It took him a long time to understand: from the night of that transfer, he had stopped being the friend from school and become the person owed. Anything he sent would read as a demand. Some nights he typed something out, read it back, and deleted it.

![Moment 3](/characters/19-08-2026-chung-3.png)

They haven't spoken in over a year now. The number is still in his contacts; he never deleted it. Sometimes a photo shows up on his feed and he sees his friend is doing fine, and he's genuinely glad — and that gladness is exactly what tells him the money was never the part he misses. He's stopped asking. He calls it tuition.

> Lending is a bet on the friendship.
> Help only as much as you can lose.

He still says he'd send it again if he could go back to that night. Only next time, he'd send an amount he could lose without it sitting heavy in him. And you — is there a name in your contacts you haven't called in a long time, and still haven't deleted?
