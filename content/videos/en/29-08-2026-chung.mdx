11pm. The phone lights up on the red plastic table: "why so late?" He picks it up, types a few words, deletes them. Not because there's anything to hide. Because the true answer sounds so small out loud.

![Moment 1](/characters/29-08-2026-chung-1.png)

These old friends only manage it at Tết and on a public holiday. Tonight they had to push two tables together to fit. They've been here since evening, and the crate under his chair is still almost full — half the bottles still capped. He can't find a way to type it so the person at home would understand: we came here to sit, not to drink. In the end he types, I'll tell you about it tomorrow.

![Moment 2](/characters/29-08-2026-chung-2.png)

What fills five hours? One of them complains about his boss. One shows off a kid who just started first grade. Then one says his mother is in the hospital — and every glass goes down at once, and nobody adds anything. They live in four different provinces now; all year they exchange a few birthday messages. This table is the only place they say the real thing. So nobody stands up to leave. They just order another plate.

![Moment 3](/characters/29-08-2026-chung-3.png)

The bill is settled, they step out to the curb, and nobody hugs anybody. Just a punch on the shoulder, then: so when's the next one? Translated properly, that sentence means *I've missed you*. It's only that none of them knows how to say it plainly. Home again, he opens his phone and saves a date for next time.

> That punch on the shoulder is the greeting.
> Still making plans means still caring.

When was the last time you sat with your old friends? And do you remember how much you drank that night — or only what they told you?
