Sunday. Wrung out, and still driving home. There's an unspoken rule in the house: Sunday
evening, everyone comes home for dinner. No excuses. Her parents, the two sisters, her
brother-in-law, the two kids — seven people around one table, week after week.

![Moment 1](/characters/25-08-2026-chung-1.png)

Nothing about the meal is special. Crab soup, pickled eggplant, braised pork. Nothing
about the conversation is either. Some nights she sits down with her head still at work,
just wanting to finish and go. Then her father says it out loud — how many more years do
your mother and I get to sit like this with you — and the table goes quiet. She puts the
phone away and stays.

![Moment 2](/characters/25-08-2026-chung-2.png)

One week she was too tired. She typed it out for her mother: I'm busy this week. Then
left it sitting there, unsent, thinking about her father's chair at the head of the
table. Her mother messaged first — she'd already bought the pickled eggplant. She'd gone
to the market that afternoon. The rule was never a weight someone put on her. It was the
thing her parents waited all week for. She deleted the message and typed a new one: I'll
be there early.

![Moment 3](/characters/25-08-2026-chung-3.png)

At the table it's all small talk. The neighbours, the price of things, her nephew getting
scolded by his teacher. She used to think none of it landed anywhere. Then the next week
her father repeated it back, every detail intact. He never needed the information. He
needed her sitting there.

> He remembers everything I tell him.
> I've never asked him anything back.

Next week she'll keep talking, like always. And his stories — maybe this time she asks first.
