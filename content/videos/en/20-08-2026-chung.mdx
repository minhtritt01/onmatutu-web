Five in the morning, the phone buzzes. A video call from my mother. I pick up and the screen shows
only the ceiling — a fan, one yellow bulb. She is somewhere below the frame, talking, while the
camera looks at nothing.

![Moment 1](/characters/20-08-2026-chung-1.png)

She has only had a phone since Tết, and she still gets it wrong. Wrong hour, camera upside down,
calls she hangs up on because she doesn't know she made them. But that 5 a.m. call was not a
mistake. She had been awake since four, sitting there for an hour, waiting until she felt sure
enough to press. The clumsiness I used to get annoyed at was just the visible part of an hour
nobody saw.

![Moment 2](/characters/20-08-2026-chung-2.png)

One trip home, I opened a drawer and found a sheet of paper. Her handwriting, shaky, one step per
line: the green circle, tap my name, tap the little camera, wait. And one more line at the bottom,
a note to herself — if you don't see the face, press again. She never asked anyone. She sat down
with her reading glasses and a ballpoint pen and copied out something that was hard for her, then
put it in a drawer so nobody would know she'd had to.

![Moment 3](/characters/20-08-2026-chung-3.png)

I snapped at her once: press the green button, how many times have I told you. She didn't argue.
She just called less after that. She wasn't angry — she was more afraid of being a bother than of
missing me. The call log thinned out, then went quiet. The cost of one sharp sentence wasn't that
she felt hurt for an evening; it was that she stopped pressing.

> She wrote every step on paper.
> The hard part, she kept to herself.

Now it goes the other way: I call first, and she answers instantly, as if the phone had been within
reach the whole time. The next time your parents call at the wrong hour, or the screen shows nothing
but a ceiling — what will you say?
