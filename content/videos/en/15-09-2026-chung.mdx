September. The desk lamp comes on and he pulls a folded sheet out of the drawer. The crease
has gone soft. Four lines written in January, the handwriting still neat — the handwriting of
someone just back from the holidays, sure this year would be different. He reads them one by
one. One done. Three half-finished.

![Moment 1](/characters/15-09-2026-chung-1.png)

The third line he actually kept up for two months before it slipped. But in his head those two
months count for nothing — not finished means quit. That's the real weight: not the three
unfinished goals, but the way he grades himself. He sits a while longer and sees something
else: four things, all at once, alone. Not lazy. Just carrying too much. He picks up a pen,
crosses out two lines. Keeps two.

![Moment 2](/characters/15-09-2026-chung-2.png)

Every year, around September, the bin under his desk gets a crumpled sheet of paper. Every
January he writes a new one — the same one. Same four goals, same order, same numbers. He
pulls the drawer open: three old lists stacked on top of each other, so alike he can't tell
which year is which. So the thing that keeps repeating isn't his failure. It's the list. This
year he doesn't tear it up. He edits it down to two lines.

![Moment 3](/characters/15-09-2026-chung-3.png)

Four months left. He cuts the number in half, and the first thing it feels like is losing. Then
he looks at the jar on the desk: the old number was never a goal, only a wish written in ink.
This one he can actually reach. He writes the new number down — smaller, truer. And somehow
it feels lighter.

> The number got smaller. He didn't.
> For the first time, he knows his own size.

How many lines are left on your list this year? And how many of them are real?
