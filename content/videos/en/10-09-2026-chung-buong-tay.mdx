Just before seven, the school gate was already crowded. She walked her daughter to the
line, crouched down to meet her eyes, and got halfway through the sentence — *be good in
class, alright*. The other half never made it out.

Six years, that small hand had fit inside hers at every crossing, every unfamiliar room.
This morning she had braced herself for the moment she would have to let it go.

![Moment 1](/characters/10-09-2026-chung-1.png)

She never got the chance. The girl let go first and ran straight in, no glance back. Her
mother stayed where she was, hand still curved around a hand that wasn't there anymore. It
turned out the one who wasn't ready was not the child.

![Moment 2](/characters/10-09-2026-chung-2.png)

The yard emptied out and she was still standing on the far side of the iron bars, nearly
twenty minutes. Her phone buzzed in her pocket, the screen lit and went dark again. She
thought she was waiting for her daughter to turn around. But the girl had been inside a
long time — those twenty minutes weren't for her. They were for getting used to it.

![Moment 3](/characters/10-09-2026-chung-3.png)

That morning she had taken out the nicest shirt she owned, the one saved for weddings,
still pressed flat from months of nobody touching it. To her it was a big day. To the
child it was an ordinary morning, and years from now she won't remember what her mother
wore.

> She let go first.
> The one who had to learn was me.

That evening she hung the shirt back up, put it away, closed the door. And your last big
day — did anyone else see how big it was?
