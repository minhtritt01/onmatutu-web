The phone is lying face-up on the desk, and the lock screen has just lit up with a bank notification. He waited a whole month for that line. Now that it's actually there, he doesn't feel much of anything — not relief, not pride. Just a number sitting on a screen.

![Moment 1](/characters/04-08-2026-van-phong-1.png)

First month in sales. Customers snapping at him, a manager pushing the quota, every single day. Then the money lands. He sits there looking at the balance and thinks something he probably wouldn't say out loud: this is smaller than the month it cost. Not smaller than anyone else's — smaller than what he put in. In the end he stops thinking about it, opens the app, and sends half of it home to his mother.

![Moment 2](/characters/04-08-2026-van-phong-2.png)

One afternoon he hid in the office bathroom. A customer had just shouted at him through the headset. Nothing dramatic, but he needed a few minutes behind a door that locked. He washed his face, walked back out, put the headset on again — and his voice was as gentle as it had been before any of it happened. That part isn't in the job description. Nobody pays for it either.

![Moment 3](/characters/04-08-2026-van-phong-3.png)

That evening his mother called. She didn't ask how much he'd earned, or how the company was treating him. She asked one thing — so you're working now — and her voice shook. He hung up and sat by the window for a while. The amount really was small. But the amount wasn't what made her voice shake.

> She never asked what the number was.
> She was glad he still had care left to give.

A payslip can measure one month of work. The part where you still get up every morning, still stay kind to people while you're running on empty — what measures that?
