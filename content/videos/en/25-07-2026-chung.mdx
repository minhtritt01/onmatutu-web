She wheels her suitcase in and opens the door to her old room out of habit. The bed is gone — her mother gave it to a neighbor a while back, and the whole room is stacked with boxes now. All that's left is a faint mark on the wall, where the headboard used to press against it for years.

![Moment 1](/characters/25-07-2026-chung-1.png)

That night she sleeps on the living-room sofa. She tosses and turns, but it isn't the stiff cushions keeping her up. Being home feels, strangely, like staying at a guesthouse — a feeling she can't quite name until she texts a friend who's also married and moved away.

![Moment 2](/characters/25-07-2026-chung-2.png)

Close to midnight, she notices her mother's light is still on. Out in the hallway, she leans in near the door and hears her mother turning over inside — awake too, maybe worried her daughter isn't sleeping well in an unfamiliar spot. She stands still and doesn't knock, just lets her be.

![Moment 3](/characters/25-07-2026-chung-3.png)

> She never says she's worried, just stays awake.
> That's her way of loving, too.

The old room may never look the same again. But that thin line of light under the door that night — maybe that's the one thing that never changed. Does your childhood home still feel the way you remember it, or is what's left really just the people who love you?
