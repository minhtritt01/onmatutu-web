Every night Mom video-called, and every night the phone pointed at the same corner of the
kitchen. Bright tiles, a pot turned upside down on the shelf, bowls stacked neatly. "See? All
clean." She nodded at the screen and believed that once the water was gone, the rest was over too.

![Moment 1](/characters/29-09-2026-chung-1.png)

Then one of Mom's neighbors posted a photo. Mattresses propped up in the yard, cardboard boxes
soaked halfway up, mud along the foot of the walls. It was Mom's house. For ten days Mom had chosen
her camera angle carefully — only the part that was still clean. She called back and said nothing
about the photo. Mom had worked hard to keep her from worrying; she didn't want Mom to feel she'd
failed.

![Moment 2](/characters/29-09-2026-chung-2.png)

This time she didn't ask what had been lost. She only asked, "Where have you been sleeping these
nights?" The line went quiet for a long moment. "On the old bamboo cot. It's nice and cool." She
bit her lip so she wouldn't cry out loud. Ten days of "I'm fine" had meant a bed that went under.

![Moment 3](/characters/29-09-2026-chung-3.png)

Mom said it was too far, don't come, the bus costs too much. She understood that didn't mean Mom
didn't need her. Mom just couldn't bring herself to say so. That night she booked a ticket home and
texted that she was coming to help dry things out. Mom sent back a sticker of a crying cat.

> Mom didn't want me spending on the bus.
> I didn't want her cleaning up alone.

The last time your mom said "I'm fine," did you ask one more question — about her?
