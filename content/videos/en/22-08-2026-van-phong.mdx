The whole floor went dark hours ago. One square of light left, and it's her desk. She's sitting in it, newly arrived at the position she spent two years waiting for, still waiting to feel lighter.

![Moment 1](/characters/22-08-2026-van-phong-1.png)

The new stack of files is half again as tall as the old one. It arrived with the title, a bundle nobody mentioned in advance. Two years ago she was out the door by six. Now she shuts the screen at eight, and those two hours don't appear in any announcement — they just quietly stop existing, a little more each day.

![Moment 2](/characters/22-08-2026-van-phong-2.png)

She opens her phone and the group chat has been going since six. She reads it, starts to reply, then doesn't. What stops her isn't the dinner she missed. It's realising they still invite her, every month, and she's the one whose thumb has learned to type *next time*. What she lost wasn't the evenings. It was the reflex to say yes.

![Moment 3](/characters/22-08-2026-van-phong-3.png)

That night she reopens a note she wrote two years ago. The first line is the goal, checked off. Under it, fainter, is a second line she'd stopped seeing: *so there's time to take my mother to her check-ups.* She reads it twice. She got the first half. She didn't keep the second.

> The milestone was never the point.
> It was how you buy the time.

That weekend she took a day off, took the keys down, and drove her mother to the clinic. Not because the position didn't matter — but because she'd just remembered who she was doing it for.
