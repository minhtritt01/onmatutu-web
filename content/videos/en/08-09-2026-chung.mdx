A September morning, on the way to work, he walks past his old university. Motorbikes parked solid along both sides of the gate, helmets still resting on the seats. Inside, a queue of first-years waiting to file their papers — every one of them with a parent in tow, faces a little lost, asking directions from anyone who'll stop.

![Moment 1](/characters/08-09-2026-chung-1.png)

He stands there a while. The school's name board has gone patchy, the paint faded so slowly nobody noticed. He thought he was looking at them. A minute longer and he knows he isn't — that lost look is his own, seven years ago, standing in this exact spot, wondering whether he could keep up at all.

![Moment 2](/characters/08-09-2026-chung-2.png)

That night he pulls the old cardboard box down off the shelf. A stack of notebooks, thick, every corner curled. Two retaken exams. A run of sleepless nights over one project. A semester he nearly walked away from, back when he was certain he wouldn't make it through. He opens the old project file, looks at it for a moment, and laughs — it reads like something somebody else did.

![Moment 3](/characters/08-09-2026-chung-3.png)

The next day, same stretch of pavement, a kid drags a suitcase over and asks where the registrar's office is. The papers in their hand are held at an angle, edges folded into each other, hands shaking a little. He points the way — the same spot where he once stood asking — and adds one more thing: take your time, everyone's a mess the first few days.

> Seven years, and I'm not much better.
> But now I'm the one who can answer.

While you're inside it, nobody tells you in advance that it passes. You only get to count it standing outside the gate, looking back. And the thing you're sure right now you won't survive — what voice will you be telling it in, a few years from now?
