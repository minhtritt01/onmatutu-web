Back home, when braised fish showed up on the table again, she used to complain. Nothing
serious — just a "fish again, mum?" and then she'd eat it anyway. Now, three months into
living alone in a rented room on nothing but takeout boxes, she's realised that the right to
complain was never free.

![Moment 1](/characters/04-09-2026-chung-1.png)

One evening she got off work early and stopped by the market for some greens and a piece of
fish. Sixty-five thousand đồng — cheaper than the takeout she'd been buying. So it was never
about the money. Standing at the stove, with the smell of braised fish rising, she finally did
the maths: her mother had cooked like that properly for eighteen years, every single day,
never skipping one. She could complain because someone always cooked. Now anything will do —
not because she has grown up, but because there is no one left looking after her.

![Moment 2](/characters/04-09-2026-chung-2.png)

Her mother still texts to ask what she's been eating. She types back "I'm eating well" while
sitting in front of a box of rice gone cold. At home there were three dishes every meal. In
three months her standard has dropped from three dishes to one box — and nobody made her do
that. It isn't that she got used to it. She let herself go. That night she cooked three
dishes, laid them out, and ate alone.

![Moment 3](/characters/04-09-2026-chung-3.png)

Two years in that room, and everything is still temporary: eating temporarily, sleeping
temporarily, then getting used to temporary. The mattress is still on the floor, the taped
box still unopened. But at home her mother never served anyone a temporary meal — not even on
her most exhausted days. She learned everything about how her mother looked after people,
except for one person.

> My mother never cooked a half-hearted meal for anyone.
> I learned all of it — except my own share.

The bowls are washed and still warm in her hands. Tomorrow she plans to cook again. And you —
when was the last time you cooked yourself a proper meal?
