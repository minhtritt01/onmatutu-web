First time home since Tết. The bus drops him at the top of the lane and he walks the rest,
suitcase in hand, road dust still on his sleeves. The door is already open, late afternoon light
reaching all the way across the floor. Then he stops for exactly one second — not because
something is new, but because something is different.

![Moment 1](/characters/07-08-2026-chung-4.png)

His father's hair has gone much greyer. His mother walks slower than he remembers. On the wall,
last month's calendar page is still hanging; nobody bothered to tear it off. Standing there, he
understands that none of this happened today. It happened a little at a time, every day, across
the whole stretch when he wasn't here. He sets the suitcase down. His hands are empty now.

![Moment 2](/characters/07-08-2026-chung-5.png)

On every video call his mother smiles. His father sits behind her, smiling too. The last thing
either of them says is always the same: we're fine, don't worry. The frame is just wide enough for
two faces and nothing else — not the weekly pill box on the table, not the cane leaning against
the wall. He believed it, because believing was lighter.

![Moment 3](/characters/07-08-2026-chung-6.png)

That evening he stays at the table longer than usual. His father talks about the neighbours, about
relatives, about nothing in particular. The teapot goes round a third time and nobody stands up.
He puts his phone face down on the table and keeps listening — because it turns out his parents
didn't need him to say anything. They just needed him still sitting there.

> Nothing special about that evening.
> There just aren't many left like it.

Nothing gets resolved here, and no promises are made out loud. Only an ordinary dinner that ran
longer than the others. When was the last time you stayed at the table like that?
