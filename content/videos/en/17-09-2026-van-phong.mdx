Does asking for a raise make you shake? It does. The night before the meeting she stands in front of the mirror, desk lamp on in one corner, a sticky note on the mirror's edge gone soft from being read too many times. Her workload has doubled since she started; her pay hasn't moved. She knows every number by heart. And still, at the word "ask," her throat closes.

![Moment 1](/characters/17-09-2026-van-phong-1.png)

A month of rehearsing, and the catch was never the figures. Her notebook has two columns: the work column crammed with lines, the pay column blank. She has it all memorized. There is just a voice in her head saying she is asking for too much — a salaried employee, making demands. That night she underlines the number three times, closes the notebook, turns off the lamp. No more rehearsing. Tomorrow she will just say it plainly.

![Moment 2](/characters/17-09-2026-van-phong-2.png)

The next day: a bench in the hallway, the boss's door shut. Her hands are ice. On her phone, a half-typed message: "Hi, could we push this back…". Walking away would feel better instantly — ten minutes and the shaking stops. But walking away means another year of silence, another year of swallowing it. Ten minutes of nerves, or a whole year of resentment. She stands up and knocks.

![Moment 3](/characters/17-09-2026-van-phong-3.png)

She says it. The boss nods: let me think about it. No answer. The door closes behind her with nothing settled. And yet on the stairs, afternoon light slanting through the window, she feels lighter. Not because she got the raise — she doesn't know yet. Lighter because the weight was never the salary. It was all the time she hadn't dared to speak. Back at her desk, her hands are warm again.

> Raise or no raise, that comes later.
> Once it's said, the resentment is gone.

And you — is there something you've earned the right to say that's still sitting in a notebook?
