Five o'clock, Sunday afternoon. The light in the window is going orange, the wall clock
reads exactly five, and something in her chest starts to fidget — nothing hurts, she's just
not settled. She knows this feeling. For three months now, every week, right on schedule.

![Moment 1](/characters/13-09-2026-chung-1.png)

That evening she leaves her rice half-eaten and watches the clock crawl from five to six.
She still calls it "being scared of Monday." But when she looks closely, she isn't scared
of the work — tomorrow's job is the same job as every day. What she's doing is counting
down. The whole week has exactly one day that belongs to her, and it's running out. So it
was never fear. It was missing something. She takes the clock off the wall, lays it face
down on the desk, and puts on some music. Tonight is still hers.

![Moment 2](/characters/13-09-2026-chung-2.png)

Some Sunday nights she can't sleep at all. Phone glowing on the pillow, work chat still
open, a nine o'clock meeting on Monday's calendar. She lies there rehearsing every way
tomorrow could go wrong. Then it occurs to her: last Monday was fine. The one before, too.
Every morning turns out okay. The bad part was never Monday — it's Sunday night, and she
builds it herself. She closes the work chat, turns the phone face down. Fearing it in
advance just means fearing it twice.

![Moment 3](/characters/13-09-2026-chung-3.png)

Another Sunday, her mother asks why she isn't eating. She says she's full — so her mother
won't worry. The bowl sits untouched, chopsticks laid across it. Her mother doesn't push.
She just slides the soup over and says: I used to be like that too. Thirty years of going
to work, and in those thirty years, who knows how many Sunday nights she sat exactly where
her daughter is sitting now. Dreading Sunday doesn't make you weak. She finishes the bowl,
and then she tells her mother everything.

> Thirty years of work, and Mom still dreads it.
> Dreads it and goes anyway. Her, then me.

The Sunday-evening restlessness doesn't go away. But once you know what it is — and know
the person across the table has carried it too — it gets lighter. This Sunday night, are
you watching the clock, or the person next to you?
